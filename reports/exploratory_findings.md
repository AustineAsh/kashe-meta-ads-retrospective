# Retrospective exploratory analysis of historical music-campaign performance

## Executive summary

This report revisits surviving Meta Ads data from my work at Kashe Music Group / Santeri, but the advertising export is only one part of the historical campaign system. A music release consumes and coordinates several forms of resource before an advertisement is ever delivered. Throsby (2006) models artistic production through inputs including labour time, physical or operating capital and human capital, while Gander's (2015) study of pop-music production shows how creative projects bring together temporarily assembled teams, material resources, knowledge and organisations under uncertainty ([Throsby, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10824-005-9001-4); [Gander, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1108/MD-03-2014-0165)). Their empirical settings are different from this campaign, so I use the concepts as a vocabulary for identifying inputs rather than as a model that can explain Bunda's outcome.

I therefore treat a release campaign as a **resource-to-response system**. The main resource domains in this case are creative and intellectual assets; specialist human capital and labour; physical, technical and operating capital; financial capital; organisational and managerial capability; network and relational resources; media/channel resources; and information or analytical resources. These resources were configured differently across stages of the artist project.

Chronology is important. Before *Let's Fly Away*, I worked within a manager-led artist-development phase. Four opening-act appearances involving Portable, two at shows he organised and two at third-party shows where he was also booked, occurred during that earlier period. I was not directing creative operations at that stage. I therefore treat those appearances as evidence of an existing artist trajectory and industry access before the later campaigns, not as outcomes produced by my subsequent creative-operations work.

*Let's Fly Away* marked the point at which I took much broader ownership of creative production and promotion. I directed and produced a visualiser, edited short-form content, developed a dance-challenge concept, coordinated creators and began using paid social more deliberately. *Bunda* was a further step-change in **production intensity and resource commitment**. The music video alone cost more than **NGN 5 million** according to my historical production records and recollection, and involved a professional set of more than 30 people including specialist production roles such as producer, director, costume/styling and scripted creative work. That figure describes an input into production, not an independent rating of artistic quality. It is more precise to say that the campaign had materially greater **production value inputs, specialist labour and operating capital** than my earlier work.

The surviving Meta export adds a documented paid-media layer to that resource picture. Eighteen campaign rows whose surviving names explicitly contain `Bunda` account for **NGN 450,035.75 of Meta spend** and **3,085,893 impressions**. Fifteen of those rows are link-click campaigns, recording **568,819 clicks from NGN 438,522.79 spend**, while three are ThruPlay campaigns recording **29,344 ThruPlays from NGN 11,512.96 spend**. Because some other campaign names are truncated or generic, I treat the explicitly named Bunda rows as a **lower-bound identifiable subset**, not a complete reconstruction of every Bunda-related ad. Combining the author-reported video-production cost with only those explicitly named Meta rows identifies more than **NGN 5.45 million** of production and Meta expenditure before other digital promotion, traditional/offline promotion, travel, live activity and other costs are added.

The campaign was also multi-channel. Lovett and Staelin (2016) distinguish **paid media** (advertising), **earned media** (word of mouth, social-media buzz and publicity) and **owned media** (brand-generated content and channels) in an entertainment-brand setting ([Lovett & Staelin, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2015.0961)). I use that classification to organise the digital side of the campaign. Owned media included the artist's social accounts, music video and short-form content; paid media included Meta, X and Google advertising; earned media included organic audience response, sharing and unsolicited interest. I keep **intermediated and offline promotion** as a separate practical category because radio, television, DJs/clubs, live appearances and industry relationships can be paid, earned or relationship-driven depending on the specific activity and do not always fit neatly into one digital-media label.

At the time, I believed that the cumulative effect of stronger content, paid distribution, organic engagement and offline promotion was producing visible market traction, and that this visibility helped when I approached radio, television and event decision-makers or negotiated opportunities. Some later opportunities were negotiated outbound and others arrived organically. I now treat that belief as a **contemporaneous managerial hypothesis**, not a finding. I did not design the campaign to test which factors drove or constrained those opportunities, and the historical data cannot isolate the effect of online engagement from production value, paid reach, prior industry relationships, song characteristics, offline promotion, platform algorithms, timing or gatekeeper preferences.

There is nevertheless contextual evidence that makes the hypothesis reasonable enough to investigate. Okolie and Onwuegbuna's (2024) descriptive study of 16 radio stations in Port Harcourt found that social-media responses were treated as a popularity indicator by the stations they studied; direct artist contact and artists' online platforms also featured in programming practice ([Okolie & Onwuegbuna, 2024](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/380345560_Evolving_Trends_in_Radio_Popularization_of_Nigerian_Urban_Music)). This does not establish why my own bookings occurred, and the study is local rather than nationally representative. It does show that online response and artist visibility can form part of the information environment used by Nigerian music gatekeepers. Polak and Schaap (2024) add a different caution: musicians often operate across creative, promotional and managerial tasks while platform success remains difficult to interpret because the mechanisms producing visibility can feel like a black box ([Polak & Schaap, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448241243095)).

Within that wider context, the Meta data allow a narrower question to be answered rigorously: **how efficiently did the surviving paid-social activity generate its recorded platform outcomes, and where was that performance concentrated?** The workbook contains **126 campaign rows**, **NGN 1,029,228.11 spend** and **10,332,896 impressions**. A separate account screenshot records **NGN 1,492,844.45 spent**, so the workbook is a partial historical export rather than a complete account record.

Across the **106 link-click rows**, the export records **635,240 link clicks** from **9,725,211 impressions** and **NGN 907,409.25 spend**, producing a portfolio-weighted **NGN 1.43 CPC**, **6.53% derived link CTR** and **NGN 93.30 CPM**. The distribution is highly uneven. Median campaign-level CPC is **NGN 19.81** and median derived CTR is **0.51%**. The two principal Bunda YouTube traffic rows and the Pressure row account for **94.32% of all recorded link clicks** while using **44.27% of link-campaign spend** and **28.00% of link-campaign impressions**. I therefore interpret the exceptionally low pooled CPC as primarily a consequence of a small number of unusually high-response campaigns rather than uniform account performance.

This distinction also clarifies what can be said about **resource efficiency**. The Meta export supports paid-media efficiency measures such as CPC, CPM and cost per recorded result. It cannot establish the efficiency of the entire Bunda investment because the total resource base is incomplete and the data do not connect production and offline costs to revenue, profit or a common downstream conversion outcome. The campaign can be described as resource-intensive and the surviving Meta component can be analysed for media efficiency; a total campaign ROI or causal return on the NGN 5 million-plus production investment cannot be reconstructed responsibly.

The value of the retrospective is therefore twofold. First, it provides evidence that the historical work involved real creative production, significant resource commitment, multi-channel promotion and paid-media activity at meaningful scale. Second, it shows how I now analyse that work differently: I distinguish inputs from outputs, paid-media efficiency from total campaign effectiveness, portfolio averages from typical campaign performance, and historical beliefs from hypotheses that would require a stronger test design.

## 1. The analytical problem: performance begins before the media metric

The previous version of this analysis started too close to the advertising dashboard. That was useful for checking the numbers, but it understated what a music release actually involves. Impressions, clicks and CPC are **media outputs**. They say little about the resources required to create the asset being promoted, coordinate the people involved, build distribution access or sustain the release across channels.

Throsby (2006) provides a useful starting vocabulary by representing artistic output as a function of inputs including **labour time, physical or operating capital and human capital**. His empirical application concerns Australian visual artists and craftspeople, so I do not import his estimated relationships into a music-video campaign. What transfers usefully is the distinction between the resources entering creative production and the outputs that subsequently emerge. Human capital includes training, experience and creative capability; operating or physical capital captures tangible productive resources; labour time captures the effort required to produce the work ([Throsby, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10824-005-9001-4)).

Gander's (2015) music-specific study makes the organisational dimension clearer. His observation of a pop-song recording project describes creative production as a process involving decisions under uncertainty, temporarily assembled teams and relationships among people, material resources, knowledge and organisations. He also shows that spatial and material arrangements help regulate tasks and roles during production ([Gander, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1108/MD-03-2014-0165)). This is closer to the practical reality of a music video or release campaign, where the output depends not only on the artist but on coordinating a temporary production system.

Drawing on these concepts and the historical work, I use the following **campaign resource map**. It is my synthesis for the case rather than a published taxonomy:

| Resource domain | What it means in a music release | Examples in the Kashe/Santeri context |
|---|---|---|
| **Creative and intellectual assets** | The artistic and communicative material being created and reused | Song/master, visual concept, script/story, artwork, hooks, captions, visualiser, music video, short-form edits, dance-challenge concept |
| **Human capital and specialist labour** | Skills, experience and labour contributed by creative, technical and commercial participants | Artist, producer, director, camera/lighting crew, editor, costume/styling, performers, content creators, manager/promotional support |
| **Physical, technical and operating capital** | Tangible and technical resources used to create and deliver the work | Studio/set, locations, cameras, lighting, audio equipment, costumes/props, editing/post-production tools, transport and logistics |
| **Financial capital** | Cash committed to acquire labour, production capability and distribution | Production fees, crew/talent costs, equipment/location costs, media spend, travel/logistics and offline promotion |
| **Organisational and managerial capability** | Capacity to combine resources into a coordinated project | Budgeting, scheduling, briefing, approvals, production coordination, release sequencing, campaign monitoring and decisions about subsequent promotion |
| **Network and relational resources** | Access created through professional relationships and prior market activity | Managers, promoters, event organisers, radio/TV contacts, DJs/clubs, collaborators, creators and other artists |
| **Media/channel resources** | Routes through which the creative product reaches audiences or gatekeepers | Artist-owned social channels, paid advertising, earned sharing/publicity, radio/TV, clubs, live performances and direct audience channels |
| **Information and analytical resources** | Data used to interpret response and make later decisions | Meta Ads Manager results, post engagement, page growth, audience messages, WhatsApp contacts and later retrospective analysis |

This map changes how I use the word **quality**. I remember Bunda as a professionally produced, higher-quality creative product, but crew size and expenditure are not themselves objective measures of artistic quality. They are much stronger evidence of **production intensity, specialisation and production-value inputs**. The historical campaign can therefore be analysed confidently in terms of the resources committed; independent assessment of creative quality would require a different form of evidence.

## 2. Media architecture: owned, paid, earned and intermediated promotion

Resource inputs do not reach an audience automatically; they are deployed through channels. Lovett and Staelin (2016) provide a useful communication classification in their study of an entertainment brand: **paid media** is advertising, **earned media** includes word of mouth, online social-media buzz and publicity, and **owned media** includes seller-generated content and channels ([Lovett & Staelin, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2015.0961)). Their empirical results concern a television programme and should not be used as effect-size benchmarks for a Nigerian music campaign. I use the classification only to organise the campaign's communication resources.

For the later Kashe campaigns, this produces the following picture:

- **Owned media:** the artist's social accounts, music video, visualisers, short-form videos, campaign copy and the direct WhatsApp audience that developed from advertising responses.
- **Paid media:** Meta Ads, X Ads and Google Ads, alongside any other paid amplification used during specific releases.
- **Earned media:** audience sharing, organic discussion, word of mouth, unsolicited interest and media/event attention that was not directly purchased.
- **Intermediated/offline promotion:** radio and television outreach, DJs and club exposure, live appearances, event networking and other traditional Nigerian promotional activity. I keep this category separate because a radio appearance, club placement or live booking can be earned, negotiated through relationships or supported by paid promotional activity depending on the case.

This matters because the campaign result I perceived at the time was cumulative. I was not experiencing Meta Ads as an isolated intervention. The audience was seeing a mixture of professionally produced assets, organic content, paid distribution and offline activity. That makes single-channel causal explanations especially difficult retrospectively.

Polak and Schaap (2024) provide a useful contemporary music-industry parallel. Based on 20 interviews with early- to mid-career musicians, they describe how musicians increasingly undertake social-media, management and other ancillary entrepreneurial work alongside the creative task itself. They also note that platform visibility is difficult for musicians to interpret because the mechanisms that make some songs or content 'work' can remain opaque, creating a perceived black box ([Polak & Schaap, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448241243095)). That problem is recognisable in my historical practice: I could observe response differences, but I did not have a design that could isolate why they occurred.

## 3. Chronology: separating existing artist trajectory from later creative ownership

A retrospective becomes misleading if later outcomes are attributed to a strategy that had not yet begun. I therefore divide the historical activity into three broad phases.

### Phase 1: manager-led artist development, before *Let's Fly Away*

During this phase I was operating primarily as the artist within a manager-led arrangement rather than directing creative operations. The four opening-act appearances involving Portable belong here: two at shows he organised and two at third-party shows where he was also booked. These appearances demonstrate that industry exposure, live-performance experience and relationship-based access already existed before I took broad creative control.

That baseline is analytically important. It means later television, radio or live opportunities cannot reasonably be attributed solely to the social-media activity that followed. Existing networks, prior performance history and accumulated reputation are plausible alternative contributors.

### Phase 2: *Let's Fly Away* and the transition to broad creative ownership

*Let's Fly Away* was the first release where I took broad ownership of creative direction and campaign execution. The work included directing and producing a visualiser, editing short-form content, sourcing royalty-cleared footage, coordinating creators, developing a dance challenge across social platforms and running paid promotion. This phase is important less because it produced the largest outcome and more because it marks the shift in my role from artist participation within a managed structure to direct responsibility for a much larger part of the creative and promotional process.

### Phase 3: *Bunda* and a higher-intensity integrated campaign

Bunda substantially increased the resource commitment. The author-reported music-video production cost exceeded **NGN 5 million**, the production involved more than **30 people**, and specialist roles included a producer, director, costume/styling and scripted creative development. Paid social then operated alongside organic/owned content and further offline/traditional promotion.

The Meta file provides a partial quantitative view of this phase. I identify **18 rows whose surviving campaign name explicitly contains `Bunda`**. Across these rows:

I therefore describe Bunda as a materially more **resource-intensive integrated release campaign**, while avoiding a total-budget figure that the surviving records cannot reproduce.

## 4. The working hypothesis I was using at the time

During the later campaign period, I perceived that stronger online engagement was changing how the artist project was received. I was getting and negotiating television, radio, live and club opportunities, and some approaches arrived organically. My working interpretation was that visible social traction, produced by the combination of creative investment, paid distribution, organic response and offline promotion, was functioning as a **market signal**. I believed it helped demonstrate that the artist had an audience and could strengthen my position when approaching or negotiating with gatekeepers.

That interpretation influenced decisions at the time, but I did **not test it**. In research terms it is better represented as a provisional theory of change:

**resource commitment and campaign execution → audience exposure and response → visible online traction → perceived audience legitimacy/market signal → potential gatekeeper interest or negotiation leverage → media/live opportunities**

The chain contains several untested links. It also sits alongside plausible alternative drivers and barriers.

### Potential drivers that were not isolated

- production value and the creative quality of the song/video;
- amount and targeting of paid media;
- organic audience response and word of mouth;
- existing manager/promoter relationships and the pre-*Let's Fly Away* artist trajectory;
- previous live performances and reputation;
- creator/collaborator involvement;
- radio, TV, DJ or event-gatekeeper preferences;
- offline promotion and direct outreach;
- timing, novelty and competition for attention;
- platform recommendation and advertising-delivery systems.

### Potential barriers that were not isolated

- market saturation and competition for limited audience attention;
- weak fit between a creative asset and the audience reached;
- opaque platform or recommendation mechanisms;
- gatekeeper preferences that were unrelated to social engagement;
- incomplete conversion and attribution tracking;
- financial and human-resource constraints;
- diminishing novelty or creative fatigue;
- gaps between online engagement, sustained fandom and commercial behaviour.

Polak and Schaap's (2024) study is relevant to the platform part of this problem because the musicians they interviewed often perceived streaming and social-media success as difficult to interpret, with creators uncertain about why some songs are picked up while others are not ([Polak & Schaap, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448241243095)). Their sample is Dutch rather than Nigerian, so I use the study to frame platform uncertainty rather than to generalise career behaviour to Nigeria.

The Nigerian radio evidence provides more local contextual support for the gatekeeper part of the hypothesis. Okolie and Onwuegbuna (2024) conducted a descriptive study of **16 of 17 radio stations in Port Harcourt**, using face-to-face interviews and observation. Ten stations treated social-media responses to songs as relevant to popularity assessment and six treated them as of minor importance; direct contact with artists was relevant to 13 stations, and artists' online platforms were relevant to 11 ([Okolie & Onwuegbuna, 2024](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/380345560_Evolving_Trends_in_Radio_Popularization_of_Nigerian_Urban_Music)). I infer from this that online response could plausibly form part of the information considered by some Nigerian radio gatekeepers. I do **not** infer that my own social metrics caused my radio or television opportunities, because this study neither examines my campaign nor separates social response from the other determinants of programming decisions.

Current Nigerian industry commentary also shows why a resource perspective matters. Pulse Nigeria's 2025 sector article describes a saturated music-marketing environment in which substantial resources flow into social campaigns, radio/TV amplification and music video, while practitioners interviewed for the article caution that high engagement can fail to convert into durable audience connection and recommend offline activations as part of the mix ([Pulse Nigeria, 2025](https://www.pulse.ng/story/the-state-of-music-marketing-in-nigeria-so-expensive-yet-little-return-2025082718333654913)). This is a journalistic sector source rather than peer-reviewed evidence, and it describes conditions after my campaign period. I use it only to show that the contemporary industry still treats resource allocation, saturation, online engagement and offline connection as interconnected practical problems.

## 5. What can be measured: effectiveness, efficiency and resource productivity

The broader campaign context makes it necessary to separate three questions that can otherwise be blurred.

**Effectiveness** asks whether an activity achieved its intended objective. A campaign designed for link clicks should be evaluated first against traffic-response outcomes, while a messaging campaign should be assessed against conversations rather than against link CPC.

**Efficiency** asks what input was required to achieve that result. Within Meta, cost per click, cost per ThruPlay and CPM are legitimate measures because spend and the corresponding platform outcome are recorded in the same system.

**Total campaign resource productivity or economic return** asks whether the combined creative, human, technical, financial and promotional investment produced sufficient downstream value. That question requires a much more complete cost base and an outcome such as revenue, profit, attributable bookings, audience lifetime value or another defined return. The surviving records do not provide that linkage.

Li, Larimo and Leonidou (2021) are useful here because their social-media marketing framework treats objectives and performance as dependent on strategic context and identifies effectiveness, efficiency and adaptiveness as important dimensions for future performance measurement ([Li et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-020-00733-3)). I use that distinction to avoid treating a cheap click as evidence that the entire release investment was economically efficient.

## 6. Source data, unit of analysis and verification

The numerical source is the `Raw Data Report` sheet in the historical workbook `30-01-23.xlsx`. The public repository contains a sanitised derivative because one campaign name in the original file includes a historical WhatsApp phone number. A separate Meta screenshot records account-level spend and is used to contextualise the coverage of the workbook.

The source contains **126 campaign rows but 96 distinct campaign-name strings**. Campaign/ad IDs are not preserved, so repeated names cannot be deduplicated reliably. I therefore use each source row as the analytical unit.

Of the 126 rows, **122 have recognisable result types**. Recalculating `amount spent / results` reproduces the exported `cost per result` value for all 122 within normal floating-point precision; the maximum absolute difference is approximately `4.95e-09`. Four rows contain an ambiguous result type/value (`2`) and are retained transparently but excluded from comparisons by result type. Those four rows account for only **NGN 126.39 spend** and **462 impressions**.

Across all rows, the workbook records **NGN 1,029,228.11 spend**, **10,332,896 impressions** and **8,630,892 summed campaign-level reach**. The account screenshot records **NGN 1,492,844.45 spent**, so the workbook captures about **68.94%** of that account-level figure and leaves approximately **NGN 463,616.34** outside the available campaign export.

I retain the summed reach value for row-level and descriptive analysis, but I do not interpret it as unique people reached across campaigns because audiences can overlap. The more stable portfolio-scale measure is therefore recorded impressions.

## 7. Paid-social delivery and traffic response

The largest comparable result group is the **106 rows reported as Link clicks**. Together these rows record:

| Measure | Result |
|---|---:|
| Spend | **NGN 907,409.25** |
| Impressions | **9,725,211** |
| Link clicks | **635,240** |
| Weighted CPC | **NGN 1.43** |
| Derived link CTR | **6.53%** |
| Derived CPM | **NGN 93.30** |

Those pooled figures are valid portfolio statistics, but the campaign distribution shows that they are not representative of a typical row. The **median campaign-level CPC is NGN 19.81**, around fourteen times the weighted CPC, and the **median campaign-level derived CTR is 0.51%**, far below the pooled 6.53%. CPC ranges from approximately **NGN 0.29 to NGN 397.11**.

That difference changed the analytical question from 'what was the account CPC?' to **which campaigns are producing that aggregate?** The two principal Bunda YouTube traffic rows and the Pressure row generated **599,178 clicks**, or **94.32% of all link clicks**, from **44.27% of link-campaign spend** and **28.00% of link-campaign impressions**.

I therefore interpret the **NGN 1.43 weighted CPC** as evidence of overall portfolio media efficiency, but not as the normal cost of a click across the historical campaign set. The median and the concentration analysis are needed to understand the distribution behind the aggregate.

## 8. Bunda and Pressure: examining the high-response cases

The two rows explicitly labelled `Bunda Youtube – official` and `Bunda Youtube` are useful because they share a clear traffic objective and dominate volume. Together they record:

| Bunda YouTube traffic measure | Result |
|---|---:|
| Meta link clicks | **565,764** |
| Impressions | **2,613,269** |
| Spend | **NGN 391,913.64** |
| Weighted CPC | **NGN 0.69** |
| Derived link CTR | **21.65%** |
| Derived CPM | **NGN 149.97** |

Their combined CPM is **higher** than the NGN 93.30 CPM across all link-click rows, while their CPC is dramatically lower than both the pooled and median CPC. The low CPC was therefore not created by unusually cheap impression delivery. Arithmetically, it reflects the much greater number of recorded clicks relative to impressions.

Pressure presents a second high-response case. Its row records **33,414 clicks from 110,215 impressions and NGN 9,825 spend**, equivalent to a derived **30.32% link CTR**, **NGN 0.29 CPC** and **NGN 89.14 CPM**. Here the CPM is close to the wider link-campaign level while the response rate is exceptionally high.

I tested the dependence of the headline metrics on these rows. Excluding the two Bunda YouTube rows leaves **69,476 clicks** and increases weighted CPC to **NGN 7.42**, while derived CTR falls to **0.98%**. Removing Pressure as well leaves 103 link-click rows at **NGN 14.02 CPC** and **0.52% CTR**. This sensitivity analysis strengthens the conclusion that Bunda and Pressure materially shape the portfolio result.

It does not establish the cause of their response. A stronger explanatory design would need comparable creative variants, consistent audiences/placements, campaign timing and downstream outcomes. Yousef, Dietrich and Rundle-Thiele's (2021) Facebook experiment provides a useful methodological contrast because it deliberately controlled creative variants through A/B testing and connected platform response with an external website action ([Yousef et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph18115954)). I use that study as a design reference, not as a performance benchmark for these campaigns.

## 9. Audience development, direct response and attention

Different campaign objectives reveal other parts of the release system.

Two `Promoting Santeri` rows record **3,977 attributed Facebook likes** from **NGN 73,259.03 spend** and **39,504 impressions**, a weighted **NGN 18.42 per attributed like**. These rows support the historical account that paid media contributed materially to audience growth during the period. The full page-growth trajectory is not contained in the export, so the recollected movement from roughly 0 to 5,000 followers within four months remains a historical account rather than a value recreated from these rows alone.

Two messaging rows record **86 conversations started** from **NGN 10,769.04 spend**, or **NGN 125.22 per conversation**. The larger click-to-WhatsApp campaign produced **78 conversations from NGN 8,460.63**, or **NGN 108.47 per conversation**. I personally engaged the incoming contacts and retained contacts for later promotion. This is important because the campaign was not only buying visibility; at least part of the paid activity was being used to create a direct audience relationship and a reusable first-party contact resource.

The attention layer is less complete. Six rows record **58,112 ThruPlays** from **NGN 20,596.01 spend**, or approximately **NGN 0.35 per ThruPlay**. Four rows record **12,859 post engagements** from **NGN 15,606.98 spend**. One separate row records **3,233 three-second video plays from 12,144 impressions**. Because the three-second views and ThruPlays relate to different sets of rows, I do not combine them into a historical retention or hold-rate measure.

## 10. What the data cannot establish about total resource efficiency

The Meta analysis is relatively strong for **paid-media delivery and response efficiency**. The wider campaign analysis is much weaker for **total return on resources**.

For Bunda, I can identify more than NGN 5 million of author-reported video-production expenditure and NGN 450,035.75 of explicitly Bunda-labelled Meta spend. I also know that additional social-media and traditional/offline promotion occurred. What is missing is a complete ledger that joins all campaign costs to comparable downstream value.

The surviving data do not consistently contain:

- purchase or streaming revenue attributable to individual campaigns;
- conversion value or profit;
- a common cost-per-booking or value-per-media-opportunity measure;
- lifetime audience value;
- all X/Google or offline promotional spend;
- the monetary value of organic publicity, TV/radio appearances or live opportunities;
- opportunity costs of the artist's and team's labour time.

Consequently I do not describe the NGN 5 million-plus production expenditure as 'efficient' simply because the campaign produced high engagement. A defensible conclusion is narrower: **the campaign used a substantially larger creative-production resource base, and parts of its Meta distribution achieved unusually efficient recorded traffic outcomes**. Whether the total campaign resource bundle generated an economically efficient return cannot be recovered from the surviving records.

## 11. What I would test now: drivers, barriers and a stronger campaign design

The retrospective converts my historical assumption into a testable research problem.

A future analysis could ask:

1. Which resource inputs are associated with stronger creative performance: production intensity, creator type, format, hook, angle, CTA, or media spend?
2. Does paid distribution amplify content that already shows strong organic response, or does it create a different response pattern?
3. Do online engagement indicators predict downstream gatekeeper outcomes such as radio/TV uptake, live bookings or direct enquiries after controlling for prior artist trajectory and existing network access?
4. Which barriers weaken the path from engagement to durable audience or commercial value?
5. What mix of paid, owned, earned and offline/intermediated activity produces the best result relative to total campaign resources?

The data structure would need to be designed before launch. I would assign stable campaign/ad IDs, tag creative characteristics, record production and promotional costs by workstream, distinguish paid and organic outcomes, maintain campaign-specific dates, and define downstream events such as landing-page visits, streams, follows, direct enquiries, bookings and revenue. Where causal questions matter, controlled tests should be used where feasible rather than inferring effects from unrelated campaign rows.

This would also address the platform-literacy problem identified by Polak and Schaap (2024). Their study shows that musicians can be highly aware of optimisation and platform pressures while remaining uncertain about what actually drives visibility. The appropriate response is not to abandon creative judgement for metrics, but to make the relationship between creative decisions, distribution inputs and outcomes more observable ([Polak & Schaap, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448241243095)).

## 12. Implications for contemporary creative analysis

Current sector discussion reinforces the need to connect creative work, resource allocation and outcomes. Nielsen (2025) argues that marketing measurement can generate large quantities of numbers without clarity when KPIs are disconnected from business priorities, while IAB UK's Measurement Hub organises measurement approaches around objectives and outcomes ([Nielsen, 2025](https://www.nielsen.com/insights/2025/why-strategy-matters-more-than-tools-roi/); [IAB UK, 2026](https://www.iabuk.com/measurement)). Pulse Nigeria's 2025 discussion of music marketing adds local practitioner context, emphasising both rising promotional costs and the danger of treating social engagement as a substitute for durable audience connection ([Pulse Nigeria, 2025](https://www.pulse.ng/story/the-state-of-music-marketing-in-nigeria-so-expensive-yet-little-return-2025082718333654913)).

For an ecommerce-style creative-analysis environment, I would extend the historical approach in three ways.

First, I would connect **creative-resource inputs** to the asset being tested: production cost, production format, creator, hook, angle, CTA, duration and production complexity.

Second, I would connect the asset to **media deployment**: paid/owned/earned source, audience, placement, spend, CPM, frequency, attention measures and traffic response.

Third, I would connect those upstream signals to **commercial outcomes**: landing-page views, add-to-cart, checkout, purchases, conversion rate, CPA/CAC, revenue, AOV, ROAS and, where appropriate, profit contribution.

That produces a much more complete decision question than 'which post got the most clicks?': **which creative and resource configuration produced the most valuable response, through which channel, at what cost, and with what degree of confidence that the observed difference is real?**

## 13. Conclusion

The surviving evidence supports a broader account of the historical work than Meta Ads activity alone. The later releases combined creative assets, specialist labour, technical production, financial commitment, management and coordination, professional networks, paid media, owned content, organic/earned response and offline/intermediated promotion.

Bunda was the clearest scale-up. The video represented more than NGN 5 million of author-reported production expenditure and a production team of more than 30 people; the surviving Meta file contains at least **NGN 450,035.75** of explicitly Bunda-labelled advertising across 18 rows. That establishes a materially higher resource commitment than the earlier campaigns, while the link-click data show that Bunda also contained some of the most efficient traffic-response rows in the account.

The later media and live opportunities are part of the historical trajectory, but I no longer treat my original explanation for them as established. I believed that online engagement and visible traction increased perceived legitimacy and strengthened outreach or negotiation, and some opportunities did arrive organically. The earlier manager-led phase, including four Portable opening slots before I took charge of creative operations, demonstrates why that explanation must remain a hypothesis: pre-existing networks and artist history were already part of the system.

The strongest conclusion is therefore not that one variable 'caused' Bunda's traction. It is that the project combined a substantially larger and more specialised resource bundle with an integrated media mix; that the surviving paid-social data show unusually concentrated response and strong media efficiency in a small number of campaigns; and that a modern analysis should explicitly test the drivers and barriers that I could only infer at the time.
